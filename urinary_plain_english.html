01

The kidney is a blood-cleaning and balancing machine

~5 min • Review questions 23, 21 • Click to expand/collapse

Plain English

The kidneys constantly check what is in your blood. They remove waste, adjust how much water and salt you keep, help control blood pressure, and help balance acid. The ureters are the delivery tubes, the bladder is the storage tank, and the urethra is the exit tube.

Picture it: Kidneys = water-treatment plant. Ureters = pipes to a tank. Bladder = tank. Urethra = exit pipe.

Translate the jargon

Renal: related to the kidneys
Cortex: outer part of the kidney
Medulla: inner region with pyramids
Renal pelvis: funnel collecting urine before the ureter
Ureter: kidney to bladder
Urethra: bladder to outside

What an exam question might mean

Male and female kidneys do the same filtering work. The main outlet difference is the urethra: the lecture describes ~3 cm in females and ~20 cm in males.

Test yourself: Is a ureter the same as a urethra?
Reveal the answer

No. Ureter carries urine to the bladder. Urethra carries it out.

Course references: Urinary System, slides 2, 7, 9, 17. Instructor review: Q23, Q21.

02

Meet the nephron: the tiny assembly line

~6 min • Review questions 17 • Click to expand/collapse

Plain English

A nephron is the microscopic working unit. Blood arrives through an afferent arteriole and enters the glomerulus, a tiny bundle of capillaries inside Bowman capsule. Filtered liquid enters the capsule, moves through the proximal tubule, loop of Henle, distal tubule, and collecting duct, then through the kidney drainage system.

Picture it: Think of a factory conveyor belt. The glomerulus is the intake filter; the tubule is the sorting conveyor.

Translate the jargon

Afferent arteriole: small blood vessel arriving at the glomerulus
Glomerulus: ball of filter capillaries
Bowman capsule: cup catching filtered liquid
PCT (in A&P): proximal convoluted tubule; early part of the kidney tube
DCT: distal convoluted tubule; later segment
Loop of Henle: loop between proximal and distal parts

What an exam question might mean

Liquid route: Bowman capsule -> proximal tubule -> Henle loop -> distal tubule -> collecting duct -> minor/major calyx -> renal pelvis -> ureter.

Test yourself: Does the blood itself flow along the tubule?
Reveal the answer

No. Filtered liquid moves in the tubule; blood remains in vessels around it.

Course references: Urinary System, slides 22, 29, 31-34, 37. Instructor review: Q17.

03

The three moves: filter, take back, add

~5 min • Review questions 1, 2 • Click to expand/collapse

Plain English

FILTRATION sends water and small substances from blood into Bowman capsule. REABSORPTION returns useful water, glucose, and salts from the tubule into blood. SECRETION moves selected substances from blood into tubule liquid for removal. These moves together create urine.

Picture it: Dump the shopping bag on a table; put the good items back in the bag; add extra garbage to the trash.

Translate the jargon

Filtration: blood -> capsule
Reabsorption: tubule -> blood
Secretion: blood -> tubule
Urea: nitrogen-containing waste
Creatinine: muscle-metabolism waste

What an exam question might mean

Expected urine contains mostly water, urea, creatinine, and varying electrolytes. Significant glucose, blood, or albumin (a major plasma protein) may signal a problem; it does not identify a diagnosis by itself.

Test yourself: If filtered glucose returns to the bloodstream, which process is that?
Reveal the answer

Reabsorption.

Course references: Urinary System, slides 40, 42, 46, 48, 53, 58-59. Instructor review: Q1, Q2.

04

The bladder: hold it, then let it go

~4 min • Review questions 18 • Click to expand/collapse

Plain English

The bladder stores urine. As it stretches, nerves trigger the micturition (urination) reflex. The detrusor muscle squeezes, the internal sphincter relaxes, and the external sphincter is relaxed when it is appropriate to urinate.

Picture it: The bladder is a stretchy balloon with a controllable outlet.

Translate the jargon

Micturition: the process of urination
Detrusor: bladder-wall muscle that squeezes
Sphincter: ring-shaped muscle controlling the outlet

What an exam question might mean

Your review asks for the number of millilitres that triggers the urge. The extracted lecture text explains the reflex but does not give a reliable exact volume: check this one with your instructor or assigned text.

Test yourself: What muscle squeezes the bladder to empty it?
Reveal the answer

The detrusor muscle.

Course references: Urinary System, slides 12, 16, 19; Instructor review Q18. Instructor review: Q18.

05

How much pressure makes filtration happen?

~7 min • Review questions 13, 14, 20 • Click to expand/collapse

Plain English

Filtration happens because blood pressure pushes fluid across the kidney filter. Other pressures push or pull back against that movement. Effective filtration pressure (EFP) is the net pushing-out pressure.

Picture it: Imagine trying to push a door open while two people push back.

Translate the jargon

EFP: effective filtration pressure: net pressure favouring kidney filtration
GHP: glomerular hydrostatic pressure: blood pressure pushing fluid out
GOP: glomerular osmotic/oncotic pressure: plasma proteins pulling water back
CHP: capsular hydrostatic pressure: capsule fluid resisting filtration
COP: capsular osmotic/oncotic pressure: normally very small in this review example
GFR: glomerular filtration rate: how much filtrate is formed over time

What an exam question might mean

Review Q14: GHP 70, GOP 30, CHP 15, COP 0 mmHg. Outward (GHP + COP) minus opposing (GOP + CHP) = 70 + 0 - 30 - 15 = +25 mmHg. The existing bank also uses a combined renal blood flow example of 1.2 L/min out of 6 L/min cardiac output = 20%; the exact course reference for that figure should be checked.

Test yourself: With GHP 70, GOP 30, CHP 15, COP 0, what is EFP?
Reveal the answer

+25 mmHg. Start with outward pressure, then subtract both opposing pressures.

Course references: Urinary System, slides 9, 42, 45; Instructor review Q13-14 and Q20. Instructor review: Q13, Q14, Q20.

06

Water: where it goes and how the body saves it

~5 min • Review questions 11, 12, 25 • Click to expand/collapse

Plain English

Water arrives when you drink and eat, and a smaller amount is made during metabolism. You lose water in urine, sweat, breathing out, and stool. If you start drying out, sensors stimulate thirst and the kidneys conserve water.

Picture it: The body has an income and expense account for water. The kidneys adjust the biggest controllable expense: urine.

Translate the jargon

Homeostasis: keeping internal conditions within working limits
ICF: intracellular fluid: water INSIDE cells
ECF: extracellular fluid: water OUTSIDE cells
Interstitial fluid: fluid in spaces between tissue cells
Plasma: liquid part of blood
Osmoreceptors: sensors noticing changes in concentration

What an exam question might mean

The fluid deck gives a broad range of 45%-75% body weight as water. The regular question bank uses 60% as an example of a non-obese adult: 70 kg x 0.60 = ~42 L. Body water varies with age and body composition.

Test yourself: Name the four exits for water.
Reveal the answer

Urine, exhaled air, sweat, and stool.

Course references: Fluid and Electrolyte Balance, slides 3-6, 12, 15, 18, 21. Instructor review: Q11, Q12, Q25.

07

The water-saving hormones without the alphabet soup

~6 min • Review questions 9, 10 • Click to expand/collapse

Plain English

ADH tells the kidneys: SAVE WATER. Aldosterone tells the kidneys: SAVE SODIUM (and increase potassium secretion), so water tends to follow. ANH tells the kidneys: LOSE MORE SODIUM AND WATER. RAAS is the hormonal chain that starts when the kidneys sense inadequate perfusion.

Picture it: ADH = save water. ALDO = save salt. ANH = send salt out. RAAS = the low-pressure alarm.

Translate the jargon

ADH: antidiuretic hormone; increases water reabsorption, lowering urine volume
ANH: atrial natriuretic hormone; increases urinary sodium loss and tends to increase urine output
RAAS: renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system
Renin: enzyme released from the kidney when renal perfusion signals drop
Angiotensin II: hormone in RAAS that helps raise blood pressure and stimulates aldosterone
Aldosterone: adrenal hormone that increases distal sodium reabsorption and potassium secretion

What an exam question might mean

Low renal perfusion -> release renin -> generate angiotensin I -> ACE converts it to angiotensin II -> aldosterone increases sodium retention. This is a physiology map, NOT an instruction to give any medication.

Test yourself: Which one directly makes the collecting duct more permeable to water: ADH, ANH, or aldosterone?
Reveal the answer

ADH.

Course references: Urinary System, slides 33, 49, 53-54, 63; Fluid and Electrolyte Balance, slides 15, 21, 35. Instructor review: Q9, Q10.

08

Sodium, potassium, and fluid problems

~6 min • Review questions 10, 22 • Click to expand/collapse

Plain English

Sodium helps regulate fluid volume and distribution. Potassium matters especially for muscles and the heart. Hyper- means HIGH; hypo- means LOW. -natremia means blood sodium; -kalemia means blood potassium; -volemia means blood volume. Edema is excess fluid in tissues.

Picture it: Hyper is the volume-up button; hypo is volume-down. -natremia = sodium; -kalemia = potassium; -volemia = circulating volume.

Translate the jargon

Hyponatremia: low blood sodium
Hypernatremia: high blood sodium
Hypokalemia: low blood potassium
Hyperkalemia: high blood potassium
Hypovolemia: low circulating fluid volume
Hypervolemia: too much fluid volume
Edema: abnormal excess fluid in tissue spaces

What an exam question might mean

If plasma proteins such as albumin are reduced, less water is held in blood vessels, which can contribute to edema. This is one mechanism, not the only one. Slide 44 lists diarrhoea under hyperkalemia; this item is flagged for instructor clarification and is NOT taught here as a general rule.

Test yourself: Translate hyperkalemia and hypovolemia.
Reveal the answer

High blood potassium; low circulating fluid volume.

Course references: Fluid and Electrolyte Balance, slides 29, 35, 37-44. Instructor review: Q10, Q22.

09

Starling pressures: why ankles get puffy

~7 min • Review questions 8, 22 • Click to expand/collapse

Plain English

Four forces affect whether water moves OUT of capillaries into tissues or IN from tissues into capillaries. Hydrostatic pressure acts like a PUSH; colloid osmotic pressure (protein pull) acts like a PULL. The side the force comes from decides the direction.

Picture it: A garden hose pushes water outward. Protein acts like a sponge that attracts water. A sponge inside the vessel pulls water inward; one outside attracts water outward.

Translate the jargon

BHP: blood hydrostatic pressure: PUSHES water OUT
BCOP: blood colloid osmotic pressure: PULLS water IN
IFHP: interstitial fluid hydrostatic pressure: PUSHES water IN
IFCOP: interstitial fluid colloid osmotic pressure: PULLS water OUT

What an exam question might mean

Course classical formula: net OUT = (BHP + IFCOP) - (BCOP + IFHP). If BHP 30, IFCOP 3, BCOP 25, IFHP 1, then (30 + 3) - (25 + 1) = +7 mmHg OUT. This is the simplified model your slides teach, not a complete modern capillary-fluid model.

Test yourself: Which two forces favour fluid moving OUT of the capillary?
Reveal the answer

BHP and IFCOP.

Course references: Fluid and Electrolyte Balance, slides 24-29. Instructor review: Q8, Q22.

10

pH and buffers: stop drowning in chemistry

~6 min • Review questions 5, 7 • Click to expand/collapse

Plain English

pH tells you how acidic or alkaline something is. A lower number means MORE hydrogen ions (H+) and more acidity. A higher number means fewer hydrogen ions and more alkalinity. A buffer is a chemical shock absorber: it stops pH swinging too far, too fast.

Picture it: A buffer is the suspension system of a car: it absorbs a bump so the passengers do not feel the whole impact.

Translate the jargon

pH: measure of acidity/alkalinity; more H+ = lower pH
Acidosis: acid-side disturbance; lecture uses blood pH below 7.35
Alkalosis: base-side disturbance; lecture uses blood pH above 7.45
H+: hydrogen ion: the acid-related particle
HCO3-: bicarbonate: the main blood buffering base
H2CO3: carbonic acid: related to dissolved carbon dioxide

What an exam question might mean

The lecture uses a normal bicarbonate-to-carbonic-acid teaching ratio of 20:1. That means 20 parts base to 1 part acid, not 20 mmol/L to 1 mmol/L as a universal patient value. A ratio of 40:2 is still 20:1.

Test yourself: If H+ increases, does pH rise or fall?
Reveal the answer

It falls (becomes more acidic).

Course references: Acid-Base Balance, slides 3, 8, 11, 15. Instructor review: Q5, Q7.

11

Three acid-control tools: chemicals, lungs, kidneys

~6 min • Review questions 15, 16, 19, 24 • Click to expand/collapse

Plain English

The first defence is chemical buffers (fast). The lungs change how much carbon dioxide (CO2) you breathe out (fast compared with kidney adjustment). The kidneys change how much hydrogen ion they get rid of and how much bicarbonate they keep (slower). Hemoglobin inside red blood cells also helps buffer hydrogen ions.

Picture it: Chemical buffer = sponge. Lungs = CO2 exhaust fan. Kidneys = slow chemical-adjustment team.

Translate the jargon

CO2: carbon dioxide
Hyperpnea: deeper/increased breathing
Renal compensation: kidney changes that help offset acid/base disturbance
Nonvolatile acids: acids that cannot simply be breathed off as CO2
Chloride shift: chloride and bicarbonate exchange involving red cells during CO2 transport

What an exam question might mean

Too acidic from metabolic causes -> breathing may increase to remove CO2; kidneys can excrete more H+ and retain bicarbonate. Too alkaline -> kidneys can reduce acid elimination and excrete more bicarbonate. Sources of fixed/nonvolatile acids in the slides include sulfuric/phosphoric acids, lactate, and ketoacids.

Test yourself: Which system can remove CO2 rapidly?
Reveal the answer

The lungs. The kidneys handle fixed acids and bicarbonate more slowly.

Course references: Acid-Base Balance, slides 5-6, 8, 12, 17-22. Instructor review: Q15, Q16, Q19, Q24.

12

Food, chloride, and the two tricky infusion questions

~6 min • Review questions 3, 4, 6 • Click to expand/collapse

Plain English

Acid-forming versus base-forming foods refers to what they leave after metabolism, not simply whether they taste sour. The lecture links acid-forming potential to minerals such as sulfur, phosphorus, and chloride. The review asks how chloride relates to alkalosis and why certain fluids may affect acid/base chemistry.

Picture it: A lemon tastes acidic, but flavour by itself does not tell you the eventual dietary acid load. Treatment decisions cannot be made from that fact.

Translate the jargon

Chloride (Cl-): negatively charged electrolyte often paired with sodium
Lactate: a metabolite that can be used in biochemical pathways involving bicarbonate
Metabolic acidosis: primary acid/base problem involving decreased bicarbonate or increased non-CO2 acid
Metabolic alkalosis: primary acid/base problem involving elevated bicarbonate relative to acid

What an exam question might mean

Your slides mention sodium lactate-containing solutions for acidosis and sodium chloride for alkalosis. Those statements are oversimplified for field treatment: type and cause of the disorder determine appropriate care. Learn the CHEMISTRY for A&P; use applicable Ontario directives and clinician supervision for actual treatment. The slide text does not supply enough detail to turn those lines into universal treatment rules.

Test yourself: Can every patient with acidosis or alkalosis be treated using one IV fluid just because the slide mentions it?
Reveal the answer

No. The class slide explains a simplified biochemical idea; clinical therapy depends on the cause, patient, and authorized guidelines.

Course references: Acid-Base Balance, slides 5-6, 25, 27-29; Instructor review Q3-4, Q6. Instructor review: Q3, Q4, Q6.
